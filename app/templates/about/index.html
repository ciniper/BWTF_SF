What It Is

This site shows all San Francisco beach water quality data in one location:

  • The water quality status for today, from the city. Can I swim, surf, or play in the water today?
  • A forecast of sewage overflows that rain causes. Was there sufficient rain to cause a sewage overflow?
  • Lab results from the city and from our volunteers. What did the city lab find? What did the Blue Water Task Force find?
  • The official history of beach postings and discharges. When did the city post each beach? When did sewage go into the water near each beach?

The Blue Water Task Force is the Surfrider water test program. Volunteers operate it. In San Francisco, our volunteers take samples at six locations every two weeks. The locations are at Ocean Beach, China Beach, Baker Beach, Crissy Field, and Aquatic Park. During storms, they take more samples. They test the samples in our own lab.

This site shows our results next to the results from the city. It also monitors the city beach map. When the city posts a beach or reports a sewage discharge, you get an alert in minutes. (A posting is a warning sign for high bacteria.)

What We Test For

We test beach water for "indicator" bacteria. These bacteria show if the water is contaminated. Contaminated water can be unsafe.

Usually, these bacteria do not cause illness. Many of them live in the human body. But they show that feces are in the water. Feces can carry viruses and bacteria that cause stomach illness, rashes, and ear or eye infections.

  • Enterococcus spp.—a group of bacteria that live in the intestines of humans and other warm-blooded animals. They stay alive for a long time in salt water. Thus, California uses them as the primary indicator for ocean beaches. On the other pages of this site, we use the short name Enterococcus.
  • Coliforms—a large group of bacteria. For many years, labs used them to examine water quality, especially in fresh water. In salt water, they die faster than Enterococcus. California sets beach limits for two types:
    • Fecal coliforms—the coliforms that come from the intestines of warm-blooded animals. They are a more direct sign of feces in the water.
    • Total coliforms—all coliforms. This includes the coliforms in soil, plants, and water, and the coliforms in animal intestines.

Where the Numbers Come From

This site uses data from city (San Francisco), state (California), federal, and volunteer sources:

  • SFPUC's live beach map — the warning signs and sewage discharge flags that the San Francisco Public Utilities Commission shows now. We examine it each minute. The Today page and the alerts use this data.
  • SF Open Data — the bacteria results from the city lab for 20 shoreline stations. The results include Enterococcus, fecal coliforms, and total coliforms. They start in July 2020.
  • The Surfrider Blue Water Task Force lab — the Enterococcus results and field notes from our volunteers. They start in September 2023.
  • State Water Board BeachWatch — all the beach advisories that San Francisco sent to the state since 1999.
  • CIWQS self-monitoring reports — all the combined sewer discharges that SFPUC reported to state regulators since 2016. CIWQS is the California Integrated Water Quality System.
  • NOAA and the National Weather Service — rain gauges, rain forecasts, and tides. The sewage overflow forecast uses this data. The Today page also shows the rain and tide data.

What the Colors Mean

Each dot on the map shows the latest status of that sampling station:

  • Safe — the city map shows that this station meets California water quality standards.
  • Caution — bacteria levels are high, but they are in the state limit.
    • The Enterococcus result is 36 MPN per 100 mL or more, but less than the 104 single-sample limit. (MPN is "most probable number." It is the standard estimate of the number of bacteria in a sample.)
  • Posted — the city (SFPUC) put a warning sign at the beach.
    • Usually, this means that bacteria levels are more than the state standards. Sometimes it is a precaution. The city takes more samples at that beach until the water is safe.
  • Discharge — the city combined sewer system overflowed into the water near this station. This usually occurs after heavy rain. The city posts the beach as a precaution. It takes samples until the water is clean.
  • Not sampled — this station has no current result. We do not know if the water is safe.

Not an Official Advisory

This is a community-science tool. Signs and notices from SFPUC or the health department are more important than this site. SFPUC gives this advice: during rain and immediately after rain, bacteria levels near the shore can be high. This can occur when there is no combined sewer discharge.

Beach Hotline: 1-877-SFBEACH (1-877-732-3224) or 415-242-2214. The city map is at webapps.sfpuc.org.
