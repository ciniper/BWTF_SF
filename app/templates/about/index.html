What it is

This site brings San Francisco's beach water quality into one place:

  • Today's water quality status, as the city reports it. Can I swim, surf, or play in the water today?
  • A forecast of rain-driven sewage overflows. Has it rained enough that a sewage overflow is likely?
  • Lab results from the city and our volunteers. What did the city's lab find, and what did the Blue Water Task Force find?
  • The official history of beach postings and discharges. How often has each beach been posted or affected by sewage, and when?

The Blue Water Task Force is Surfrider's volunteer-led water testing program. In San Francisco, our volunteers sample six locations across Ocean Beach, China Beach, Crissy Field, and Aquatic Park every two weeks, add extra rounds during storms, and run the samples in our own lab. This site puts those results side by side with the city's, and watches the city's beach map so you hear about a posting (a warning sign for high bacteria) or a sewage discharge within minutes.

What we test for

Beach water is tested for "indicator" bacteria, or bacteria that indicate whether water is contaminated and potentially unsafe to enter. While these bacteria aren't usually harmful on their own—and many are actually naturally occurring within our bodies—they do signal fecal contamination, which can carry viruses and bacteria that cause stomach illness, rashes, and ear or eye infections.

  • Enterococcus spp.—a group of bacterial species that live in the intestines of humans and other warm-blooded animals. They survive well in salt water, which makes them the main indicator California uses for ocean beaches. For brevity, the rest of the site refers to them as Enterococcus.
  • Coliforms—a large group of bacteria that have long been used to check water quality, especially in fresh water. They die off faster in salt water than Enterococcus, but California still sets beach limits for two measures:
    • Fecal coliforms—a subset of coliforms that come specifically from the intestines of warm-blooded animals, pointing more directly to fecal contamination.
    • Total coliforms—all coliforms, spanning those found in soil, plants, and water as well as in animal intestines.

Where the numbers come from

This site pulls together data from city (San Francisco), state (California), federal, and volunteer sources:

  • SFPUC's live beach map — the warning signs and sewage discharge flags that the San Francisco Public Utilities Commission is showing right now. We check it every minute, and that data powers the Today page and the alerts.
  • SF Open Data — the city lab's bacteria results (Enterococcus, fecal coliforms, and total coliforms) for 20 shoreline stations, going back to July 2020.
  • Surfrider's Blue Water Task Force lab — our volunteers' Enterococcus results and field notes, going back to September 2023.
  • State Water Board BeachWatch — every beach advisory San Francisco has filed with the state since 1999.
  • CIWQS self-monitoring reports — every combined sewer discharge SFPUC has reported to state regulators since 2016, from the California Integrated Water Quality System.
  • NOAA and the National Weather Service — rain gauges, rain forecasts, and tides, which feed the sewage overflow forecast and the current conditions shown on each beach.

What the colors mean

Each dot on the map shows the latest status for that sampling station. Here's how to read them:

  • Safe — the city's map shows this station meeting California's water quality standards.
  • Caution — bacteria levels are elevated but still within the state limit.
    • Specifically, an Enterococcus result of at least 36 MPN per 100 mL but below the 104 single-sample limit. (MPN, or "most probable number," is the standard estimate of how many bacteria are in a sample.)
  • Posted — the city (SFPUC) has put up a warning sign at the beach.
    • This usually means bacteria levels are above state standards, though sometimes it's a precaution. The city will keep resampling that beach until the water tests safe.
  • Discharge — the city's combined sewer system has overflowed into the water nearby, usually after heavy rain. The beach is posted as a precaution and sampled until it clears.
  • Not sampled — there's no current result for this station, meaning water safety is unknown.

Not an official advisory

This is a community-science tool; posted signs and notices from SFPUC or the health department take precedence. SFPUC's own advice: during and immediately after rainfall, nearshore bacteria concentrations may be elevated, even when there has not been a combined sewer discharge.
